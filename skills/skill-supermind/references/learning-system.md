# Human Learning System

The goal is not to store more information. It is to improve the rate at which you can retrieve, explain, connect, and apply useful knowledge.

## A compact external brain

Use four layers:

1. **Working memory:** one active question and the current artifact. Keep this visible.
2. **Compressed notes:** a one-page explanation in your own words, with a diagram or example.
3. **Connected notes:** links to contrasts, prerequisites, applications, and disputes.
4. **Retrieval archive:** questions and small answers revisited after increasing intervals.

Your brain should not hold the catalog. The system should hold stable external representations and return only what the current task needs.

## The 60–90 minute learning block

Use [daily-learning-loop.md](../assets/daily-learning-loop.md).

1. **Orient — 5 minutes.** Write the outcome and one test of understanding.
2. **Orient attention — 10 minutes.** Skim headings, figures, and prior questions. Do not highlight everything.
3. **Learn selectively — 20–30 minutes.** Read the smallest section that answers the active question. Take notes in your own words.
4. **Make it concrete — 10 minutes.** Work one example, draw one model, or change one variable and predict the result.
5. **Connect — 10 minutes.** Compare with one known idea, identify a boundary, and link one adjacent skill.
6. **Retrieve — 10 minutes.** Close the source. Explain the idea, solve the example, and write what failed.
7. **Compress — 5 minutes.** Produce a five-line summary and one open question.

Stop if you can explain the idea, produce or recognize a valid example, state a boundary, and recall the key mechanism later. Reading longer is not the target.

## Daily decision filter

Before adding material, ask:

- Does this answer an active question?
- Will it change a decision, prediction, or artifact?
- Can I find the smallest sufficient source?
- Can I state the claim in one sentence?
- Can I test whether I understood it?
- Where will it connect to existing knowledge?

If the answer to most is no, defer it. A reading list without an active question is often procrastination disguised as preparation.

## Spaced retrieval

After a session, answer its core questions without looking. Revisit after increasing delays—for example the next day, three days, one week, and several weeks. Adjust to the difficulty and importance of the material.

A review should produce an answer, not simply reread the source. Mark:

- **Recall:** retrieve the idea.
- **Understand:** explain mechanism and boundary.
- **Apply:** solve a novel example.
- **Teach:** give a concise, accurate explanation.
- **Diagnose:** identify why a wrong approach fails.

Weakest level determines the next review.

## Interleaving

Mix related problem types only after understanding each one separately. For example, alternate between deriving a formula, interpreting a graph, debugging a circuit, and choosing a tool. Interleaving is not random distraction: the categories must be discriminable and relevant.

## Expert practice

Experts do more than recall facts. They:

- decompose a novel problem into familiar structures;
- notice constraints and high-leverage variables early;
- predict before calculating;
- compare a near miss against a known case;
- use external representations to offload working memory;
- create feedback loops with fast, trustworthy signals;
- update beliefs when evidence contradicts them;
- automate stable, repetitive operations;
- preserve exceptions and failure modes, not just the happy path.

For AI, systems, and hardware, use a loop:

```text
specify → model → predict → build/simulate → measure → explain error → update model
```

The explanation step prevents blind iteration. Record units, assumptions, tolerances, uncertainty, and the exact revision tested.

## Anti-fragmentation rules

- One active hard problem per deep-work block.
- Keep a parking lot for tangential ideas.
- Use checkpoints so notes remain recoverable after interruption.
- Prefer one integrated artifact over dozens of isolated highlights.
- Revisit links only when they resolve a current need.
- Stop adding systems when the current bottleneck is execution, evidence, or sleep.

## Energy and attention

Use the day’s hardest work when your attention is strongest. Reserve low-cognitive-demand time for routine capture and review. Protect sleep and recovery; fatigue degrades attention, judgment, and consolidation. Breaks and movement help when used to restore attention, not to avoid difficult work.

These are general habits, not medical advice. If fatigue, mood, sleep, or attention problems are persistent or impair daily functioning, consider a qualified health professional.

## Weekly consolidation

Use [weekly-review.md](../assets/weekly-review.md):

1. Retrieve the week's core ideas without notes.
2. Keep the few ideas that changed your model or decisions.
3. Merge duplicates and split overloaded notes.
4. Convert open questions into experiments or source searches.
5. Schedule the next reviews.
6. Delete tooling or structure that did not reduce friction.

The system should become smaller and clearer as knowledge grows.
